One letter, one family, the 49th blood group

How a pan-reactive antibody in a pregnant woman led to JAMA (ISBT 049), a blood group carried on an adhesion molecule most of us know from platelets. Bench to bedside, told as a case.

Dr Abdul Mannan · Consultant HaematologistSource: Mankelow, Tilley, Karamatic Crew et al. Vox Sang 2026~12 min read
At a glance

Clue, proof and limits

  1. 1 · The family clue

    Everyone reacts, except two siblings

    • Pregnant woman, no known transfusion history
    • Moderate pan-reactive antibody by LISS IAT, first investigated in 2013
    • Still unresolved by reference serology in 2020
  2. 2 · The molecular proof

    One amino acid on D2 of JAM-A

    • Both siblings homozygous for F11R c.644G>A, p.Arg215Gln (exon 6)

    What was shown, and what was not

    Shown

    • JIMI-negative cells still express JAM-A, at lower levels

    Not shown or not known

    • Haemolytic transfusion reactions
    • HDFN risk beyond this one family
    • Platelet refractoriness or FNAIT
    • How common JIMI-negative people are

Lens 1 · The story

A woman nobody could match

In 2013 a blood service laboratory in Israel received a sample from a pregnant woman of Arab ethnicity. She had never been transfused. Her plasma held an antibody of moderate strength that reacted by indirect antiglobulin test with every red cell the team tried. Every cell except two sets: her own and her brother's.

That pattern tells an immunohaematologist a lot. An antibody that hits everyone except the patient and a sibling points to an inherited absence of a high-prevalence antigen. Siblings can inherit the same two copies of a rare variant from their parents. The job was simply to name the missing antigen.

It would not be named. The samples went to the International Blood Group Reference Laboratory in Bristol. The antibody reacted with cells from a large panel of rare null phenotypes, so no known "missing antigen" explained it. The siblings' cells reacted with an extensive set of rare antisera against high-prevalence antigens, so they were not negative for any antigen we already knew about.

Seven years later, in 2020, she was pregnant again. New samples, same result, still no answer. By then the tools had changed. Whole-exome sequencing had already cracked several old puzzles in red cell serology, and the Bristol team turned it on this family.

One variant survived their filters. It sat in a gene nobody had linked to a blood group before: F11R, which makes JAM-A. The acknowledgements of the paper carry a lovely detail. Dr Frances Spring had long believed that JAM-A would carry a blood group one day. She was right.

"An antibody that reacts with everyone except you and your brother is your family history written in plasma."

Lens 2 · The science · Bench

Meet JAM-A: the protein behind the new group

What JAM-A is. JAM-A belongs to the immunoglobulin superfamily. Picture a short arm sticking out of the cell. It has two "knuckles" outside the cell, called Ig domains. The outer one (D1) is a V-set domain at the N-terminus. The one nearer the membrane (D2) is an I-set domain. Then comes a single pass through the membrane and a short 39-amino-acid tail inside the cell.

Where it lives. JAM-A is expressed on several cell types, including epithelial and endothelial cells (where it sits in tight junctions), platelets, red cells and other blood cells. It has even been described as a marker of long-term repopulating haematopoietic stem cells.

What it does. JAM-A likes to hold hands. Two molecules on the same cell pair up side by side (a cis dimer, through the R59-V60-E61 motif on D1). Molecules on neighbouring cells grip each other across the gap (a trans interaction, through N43-N44-P45, also on D1). D2 is the docking site for the leucocyte integrin αLβ2 (LFA-1).

Why haematologists already knew it. In platelets, JAM-A keeps the fibrinogen receptor αIIbβ3 switched off. Its tail holds Csk kinase, which keeps the integrin inactive. When the platelet is activated, the tail gets phosphorylated, Csk lets go, and αIIbβ3 can switch on. JAM-A knockout mice show thrombotic and other problems.

What it does on red cells. Nobody knows yet. The authors suggest it may help hold erythroblastic islands together during red cell production, the same role proposed for the red cell adhesion molecules BCAM (Lutheran) and ICAM-4 (LW).

The one-letter change

Codon 215 of F11R spans nucleotides 643 to 645. The variant swaps the middle letter, G to A. That turns CG· (arginine) into CA· (glutamine). Arginine carries a positive charge on a long side chain. Glutamine is similar in size but neutral. Modelling on the crystal structure shows both side chains pointing out into the plasma, and the authors describe the change as conservative, with little structural alteration.

Analogy

JAM-A is a name badge every red cell wears. JIMI-positive people have one letter printed as "R". This family's badge says "Q". Same badge, same shape, one letter different. Her immune system read that letter and called it foreign.

Mechanism

Homozygous p.Arg215Gln removes the Arg215 epitope from the surface of D2. The protein is still made and still reaches the membrane, at lower levels on the siblings' mature red cells. She made an alloantibody against Arg215 JAM-A; the authors report that it formed during pregnancy.

Consequence

Her antibody reacted with every red cell tested apart from the siblings'. Because JAM-A sits on platelets too, the antibody bound platelets as well as red cells in the laboratory. Whether that matters clinically is not yet known.

A detail worth holding on to

JIMI-negative is not JAM-A null

A monoclonal anti-JAM-A still labelled the siblings' red cells by flow cytometry. Their expression was lower than all eight controls, and the patient had the lowest level of all. The authors report that Arg215 and Gln215 JAM-A were expressed equally in CHO and HEK cells (data not shown in the paper). Their explanation is that the lower level on red cells arises from membrane remodelling during the last stages of red cell maturation, rather than from how the protein is made. This is a hypothesis; it was not tested directly.

The detective work · How they proved it

Six steps from "unknown antibody" to a new system

Finding a rare variant is not enough. The ISBT needs proof that the antibody binds the protein, and that it binds the protein because of that one amino acid. Here is the chain the team built, in the order they needed it.

  1. 1

    Serology

    Is this an antibody to a known antigen?

    LISS tube IAT, moderate strength, all cells positive except the siblings'. An active eluate was incompatible with a large panel of rare null cells. The siblings' cells were positive with every rare antiserum tried.

    Answer: no known specificity
  2. 2

    Enzyme and chemical profile

    What kind of structure carries the antigen?

    Resistant to papain, trypsin, pronase and DTT. Destroyed by chymotrypsin. A useful fingerprint that matched nothing on file.

    Answer: a chymotrypsin-sensitive protein epitope
  3. 3

    Whole-exome sequencing of both siblings

    Which gene is different?

    No variants in known blood group genes explained it. Filters kept shared, rare (<0.01), homozygous coding variants that change a surface-exposed part of a red cell membrane protein. Only one variant passed: F11R c.644G>A, p.Arg215Gln.

    Answer: a candidate in JAM-A
  4. 4

    Flow cytometry on red cells

    Is JAM-A really on red cells, and does the patient's plasma behave like anti-JAM-A?

    Mouse anti-JAM-A labelled all 8 control samples and, more weakly, the siblings' cells. Patient plasma labelled controls in the same way but did not bind the siblings' cells.

    Answer: yes, and the pattern fits
  5. 5

    Recombinant JAM-A: the decisive test

    Does the antibody see residue 215?

    Both forms were built as soluble Fc fusion proteins. Arg215 Fc inhibited the patient's antibody; Gln215 Fc and a Lutheran Fc control did not. Mouse anti-JAM-A was inhibited by both forms, so both proteins kept the region that antibody binds. The authors took this as evidence that both were biologically active. On transfected CHO cells, the patient's eluate bound only Arg215 JAM-A cells.

    Answer: the epitope is Arg215
  6. 6

    Platelet adsorption and elution

    Does the antibody bind the same protein on platelets?

    Washed control platelets adsorbed the antibody from plasma. The eluate from platelets agglutinated control red cells but not the patient's. An anti-D control was not adsorbed, since platelets carry no RhD.

    Answer: yes, red cells and platelets

On step 5
Lens 3 · The stumbling block

Why this took seven years

The antibody was never going to fit. Serology works by comparison. You match the antibody's pattern against cells and antisera that define antigens we already know. When the antigen belongs to a protein no one has linked to a blood group, every comparison comes back blank, and the case sits in the freezer.

The genetics held a second trap. Most variant pipelines rank changes by how badly they damage a protein. By that yardstick, p.Arg215Gln looks harmless. PolyPhen-2 called it benign (0.021). SIFT called it tolerated (0.76). AlphaMissense called it likely benign (0.112). A team hunting for "damaging" variants could have thrown it away. These tools estimate the likely effect of a change on protein function. They do not assess whether a surface amino-acid change can be seen by another person's immune system as an alloantigen. Nor did the study test JAM-A function directly. The Bristol filters asked a different question: is the change on the outside of a red cell membrane protein? That is the question that matters for a blood group.

Cognitive trap

Anchoring on the known list. Assuming a pan-reactive antibody must target one of the existing systems, and repeating serology rather than changing the method.

False reassurance

"Benign" in silico scores. Variant-effect tools estimate effects on protein function. They do not assess whether a surface change can act as an alloantigen.

The test that saved it

Whole-exome sequencing of two affected siblings, filtered for shared homozygous, surface-exposed variants in red cell membrane proteins.

Alternatives considered

Antibodies to known high-prevalence antigens: argued against by the rare null panel and rare antisera. Autoantibody: her plasma did not react with her own cells, which supports an alloantibody, though a negative autocontrol alone does not exclude every autoantibody.

Red flag for the lab

The sibling pattern is the clue

A pan-reactive alloantibody with a compatible sibling points to an inherited missing antigen. If the specificity stays unresolved after reference serology, that is the moment to think about sequencing both affected relatives, not the moment to file it as "unidentified".

Bedside · What it means for patients

What we know, and what we do not

QuestionWhat the paper shows
Was HDFN seen?No HDFN was reported in four live births from six pregnancies. The paper does not link the two pregnancy losses to the antibody. One family cannot define the risk.
Can it cause a transfusion reaction?Unknown. Neither sibling has been transfused.
Were the siblings unwell?No associated pathology was reported. Both had normal Hb, MCV, MCH, RDW, platelet count and MPV. This fits with, but does not prove, normal JAM-A function.
Why does platelet binding matter?The authors say any anti-JAM-A must be considered for effects on red cells and platelets, including fetal and neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia and platelet or granulocyte transfusion. Platelet binding was shown in vitro. FNAIT, platelet refractoriness and transfusion reactions were not shown.
How rare is the negative type?The reported gnomAD global allele frequency is 0.0000044. That describes the variant allele, not the number of JIMI-negative people, and the paper does not estimate how common the negative phenotype is.
Same protein, different cell

An antigen can look different on different cells

The authors point to a warning from a neighbouring system. Csa on red cells and HNA-3a on neutrophils are defined by the same amino acid in CTL2. Yet anti-HNA-3a does not bind red cells, while anti-Csa binds both. CTL2 has a small extracellular part buried among other membrane proteins. JAM-A holds its two Ig domains well clear of the membrane, which may be why anti-JIMI binds red cells and platelets alike.

Practical points for a transfusion laboratory

These are general principles for antibodies to high-prevalence antigens, applied to JAMA. They are my suggestions, not recommendations from the paper.

  • Refer any unresolved pan-reactive alloantibody with a negative autocontrol to a national reference laboratory. In the UK, that is IBGRL via NHSBT.
  • Consider siblings early. They may be a useful first source for donor investigation, but compatibility and donor suitability still have to be assessed.
  • Plan pregnancies and elective surgery ahead with the blood service. Rare-donor registries and frozen rare units take time to mobilise.
  • If a JIMI-negative mother's baby is thrombocytopenic, keep the platelet question open and discuss it with the platelet immunology service.
Talking to the patient

How I would explain it in clinic

"Your blood has a very rare type. Almost everyone carries a small marker on their red cells, and yours do not have it. At some point your body made an antibody against that marker. The main practical effect we know about is that finding matching blood for you is harder, because most donors carry the marker. In the study, there was no sign of the antibody causing blood problems in four babies born alive, which is reassuring. But only one family has been studied, so we do not yet know all the possible effects. If you need a transfusion, an operation or care in a future pregnancy, tell your team early, so they can involve transfusion specialists and plan ahead."

Lens 4 · The stamp

Five lines to carry for life

A variant scored benign can still create a blood group, because pathogenicity tools ask whether the protein works, not whether someone else's immune system can see the change.

JIMI-negative cells still carry JAM-A, because the variant alters one surface residue rather than deleting the protein, so a monoclonal anti-JAM-A will still label them.

The proof came from the patient's own plasma: Arg215 JAM-A blocked it and Gln215 JAM-A did not, while a mouse antibody was blocked by both.

Anti-JIMI bound platelets as well as red cells in the laboratory, because JAM-A sits on both, so effects on fetal platelets and platelet support stay open questions until shown.

When an antibody reacts with everyone except a sibling, the family is the clue, and siblings are a sensible first place to look for donors, though compatibility and suitability still have to be assessed.

The bigger picture

A decade of new blood groups

Before JAMA there were 398 accepted blood group antigens. 371 had a known genetic basis and sat in 48 systems. Nine of those 48 were found in the last five years alone. The authors count 12 other new systems ratified in the past decade, and a pattern is emerging: several of the newest blood groups sit on proteins we first knew from other blood cells. CD36 on platelets. Mal on leucocytes. CTL2 on granulocytes. And now JAM-A. All blood cells share a common precursor, so the overlap makes sense.

Recent relatives, from the same laboratory and its collaborators (as cited in the paper):

EMP3 · MAM (2020) PIEZO1 · Er (2023) SLC44A2 / CTL2 · Cs and HNA-3 (2023, 2025) MAL · AnWj (2024) F11R / JAM-A · JAMA (2026)
Two errors to avoid in JAMA diagrams

First, JAM-A has two extracellular Ig domains, D1 and D2. Drawings with three extracellular beads are wrong. Second, the paper does not show platelet refractoriness, FNAIT or transfusion reactions. The authors list platelet and granulocyte transfusion and FNAIT only as areas to consider, so present them as open questions.
